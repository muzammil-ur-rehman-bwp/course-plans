# Lab Notes 14 — Open Problems Critical Writing

**Concept recap:** a genuinely open problem has a specific, identifiable technical obstruction
remaining even after real attempts — "nobody has tried yet" is not the same claim.

**Common pitfalls:**
- In Task C, stating the obstruction too vaguely ("it's hard") rather than naming the specific
  technical reason progress has stalled (e.g., "no known routing mechanism provides a provable
  load-balance bound without an auxiliary loss's extra hyperparameter, because the routing
  decision itself is discrete and non-differentiable at the assignment boundary").
- Confusing Task B (what is known) with Task C (what is unknown) — a common mistake is restating
  the known partial result in Task C instead of stating what specifically remains beyond it.
- In Task D, forcing a connection to your capstone interest that is not genuinely there — it is a
  perfectly good answer to state that the chosen open problem does not change your current
  direction, provided that conclusion is reasoned rather than simply asserted.
- Writing well past the suggested word counts — this exercise rewards precision and concision,
  not exhaustive coverage; a tightly-reasoned 300 words is stronger than a diffuse 600.

**Debugging tip:** if you are unsure whether your Task C obstruction is "genuine" or just
"currently inconvenient," ask what specific new technique, proof, or experiment would resolve it
— if you cannot name one, the obstruction may not yet be stated precisely enough.

**Instructor tip:** when reviewing submissions, check specifically whether Task D's stated
relation to the capstone is substantive (actually shapes or constrains the eventual problem
statement) or merely nominal ("this topic is in the same general area") — press students toward
the former in feedback.
