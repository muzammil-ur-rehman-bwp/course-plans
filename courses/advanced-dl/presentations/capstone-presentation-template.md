# Capstone Presentation Template (Student-Facing)

**Target length:** 12–15 minutes + extended committee-style Q&A (qualifying-exam/thesis-proposal-
defense format, not a conference talk) | **Given to students in Week 8 alongside the capstone
proposal guidelines, used for the Week 15 peer-review-workshop practice talk and the Week 16
defense.**

## Required Slides
1. **Title** — your proposal's working title, your name, and a one-line statement of which
   course topic it belongs to (e.g., "Preference-Based Alignment Engineering").
2. **Problem statement** — the precise, falsifiable question your proposal investigates. State
   it so a knowledgeable reader could say, after this slide alone, what evidence would answer it.
3. **Related work** — your 5+-paper survey, compressed to the key prior results and how they
   relate to each other, ending with the specific gap your proposal targets.
4. **Proposed novel approach** — what you are proposing to do, and why it is distinguishable
   from simply restating any one surveyed paper's contribution.
5. **Feasibility argument or preliminary results** — if you have a pilot result, report it
   honestly, with multiple-seed spread for any stochastic method, not a single cherry-pickable
   number. If you do not, state explicitly what must be true for your approach to work.
6. **Anticipated risks & limitations** — name the single most likely way your approach could
   fail, honestly and without minimizing it, and give your reasoned case for why the risk does
   not make the proposal obviously doomed.
7. **Committee Q&A** — invite questions and be ready to defend the problem statement's precision
   and the survey's accuracy under direct, possibly skeptical, questioning.

## Presentation Tips
- This is a **qualifying-exam-style defense**, not a conference talk reporting finished work —
  you are being evaluated on the soundness of a plan for research not yet completed (the Week 13
  standard), so a rigorous feasibility argument with an honestly named risk can outscore a
  flashy preliminary result attached to a weak survey or a vague problem statement.
- Lead with the question, not the topic — the committee should understand, within the first two
  minutes, exactly what you are proposing to find out and why the existing literature (your
  survey) leaves it open.
- State your survey's gap explicitly on slide 3 itself — do not make the committee infer it from
  the list of papers.
- Name your approach's real risk on slide 6 before anyone asks about it — a proposal that only
  lists reasons it will work has not met the Week 13 feasibility-argument standard and will be
  pressed on this in Q&A regardless.
- In Q&A, "the survey doesn't settle that, and here's why I think it's still open" is a stronger
  answer than an overstated guess — this directly reflects the academic-integrity standard in
  `course-plan.md` §10 and is part of the oral-defense component of `assignments/capstone-rubric.md`.
- Practice the timing: 12–15 minutes plus committee Q&A goes faster than it sounds for a proposal
  with four required components — cut detail, not the problem statement or the named risk.
