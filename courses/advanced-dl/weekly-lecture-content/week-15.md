# Week 15 — Lecture Content: Research Proposal Work Session

## 1. Purpose of This Week
There is no new technical content this week — the graded skill is applying everything from Weeks
1–14 to your own capstone proposal, and giving/receiving the kind of structured, critical feedback
a thesis-proposal committee would give, before the Week 16 defense.

## 2. The Four Review Questions
Every peer review (and every self-review before submitting) should answer these four questions
specifically, with reference to the draft's actual text — not as a general impression:
1. **Is the problem statement precise and genuinely open?** Apply the Week 13 precision test: can
   a reader state, after reading only the problem statement, what evidence would resolve it? Is
   it a question, not a restatement of a topic? Is it not already settled by material this course
   covered or by the student's own survey?
2. **Does the related-work survey correctly represent and motivate from the cited papers?** Does
   it accurately state what each paper claims and found (not a paraphrase of its title/abstract)?
   Does it relate papers to each other (who builds on whom, where they agree or conflict)? Does it
   end by using the survey to motivate the specific gap named in the problem statement?
3. **Is the proposed approach genuinely distinguishable from the surveyed work?** Is it the
   student's own formulation — possibly combining or extending existing ideas — rather than a
   restatement of one surveyed paper's contribution as if it were novel?
4. **Is the feasibility argument honest about risk?** Does it state explicitly what must be true
   for the approach to work, name the single most likely failure mode honestly (not minimized),
   and give a reasoned case for why that risk does not make the proposal obviously doomed? A
   feasibility argument that only lists reasons the approach will work has not met this standard.

## 3. Running the Peer-Review Workshop
Work in small groups (3–4 students). Each student's draft proposal is reviewed by the other group
members using the four questions above; reviewers write specific, itemized feedback (not "looks
good" or "needs work" — a specific sentence or section, and what exactly is missing or unclear).
The author then has a short window to ask clarifying questions, in the spirit of a real committee
Q&A, before the group moves to the next draft.

## 4. From Feedback to a Revision Plan
After receiving feedback, write a short, itemized revision plan: for each piece of feedback
received, state (a) whether you agree with it, (b) if so, exactly what you will change and where,
and (c) if you disagree, why — a thesis-committee-style response, not a vague acknowledgment.
This revision plan itself is part of the Week 15 deliverable and is the direct input to your
final Week 16 submission.

## 5. Looking Ahead to Week 16
The final defense (Week 16) follows the same four-question structure, now under live,
possibly-skeptical committee questioning rather than written peer feedback. Use this week's
workshop as a dry run: if a question stumped you here, expect it again in Week 16, and prepare an
honest, specific answer rather than an overstated one — "the survey doesn't settle that, and
here's why I think it's still open" is a stronger answer in both settings than a confident guess
the evidence does not support.

## 6. In-Class/Lab Exercise
Complete at least one full round of the peer-review workshop (as both author and reviewer) using
the four review questions, and submit a written, itemized revision plan responding to every piece
of feedback you received, specifying exactly what you will change before Week 16's final
submission.
