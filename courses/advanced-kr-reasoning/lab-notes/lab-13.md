# Lab Notes 13 — Structured Paper Critique and Related-Work Annotation

**Concept recap:** reading order (abstract → results → formal definitions → related work → full
read) front-loads the question of whether the argument actually establishes the claim; the
precision test for a problem statement asks whether a reader can state, from the statement alone,
what evidence would resolve it; a survey annotation records each paper's actual claim and
finding, not its abstract paraphrased.

**Common pitfalls:**
- In Task A, restating the paper's *topic* as its "central claim" (e.g., "the paper is about
  probabilistic description logics" is a topic, not a claim — a claim is a specific, checkable
  assertion the paper argues for).
- In Task B/C, writing a problem statement that is actually a restatement of an entire open
  problem from Week 14's survey (too large) or something already fully resolved by this course's
  own material (too small) — the precision test alone does not catch scope problems, only
  vagueness; both checks are needed, and this week focuses on precision, with scope-checking
  deferred to Week 14.
- In Task D, annotating a paper by its abstract's own wording rather than reading enough of the
  actual paper to state its finding independently — a telltale sign is an annotation that could
  have been written without opening the paper at all.

**Debugging tip (for writing, not code):** read your own Task B problem statement out loud to
someone outside the course; if they cannot tell you what kind of evidence would settle it, it is
very likely still a topic in disguise.

**Instructor tip:** in the Task C peer-review rotation, explicitly forbid the reviewer from
asking the author "what did you mean?" — the reviewer must work only from the written statement,
exactly as a real reader (or the Week 16 committee) would, which is the whole point of the
exercise.
