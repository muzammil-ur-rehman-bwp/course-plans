# Lab Notes 14 — Open-Problem Connection Essay

**Concept recap:** this week's open problems are a snapshot of an actively-moving research area,
not settled fact; a capstone problem statement should sit between "too large" (a restatement of
an entire open problem) and "too small" (already resolved by this course's own material).

**Common pitfalls:**
- In Task A, restating the lecture content's own obstacle description nearly verbatim instead of
  genuinely restating it in different words — the point of "in your own words" is to verify
  understanding, not test memorization; a near-identical restatement suggests the underlying
  obstacle was not actually understood well enough to paraphrase.
- In Task B, naming a partial approach that is actually just a *restatement* of the obstacle
  itself (e.g., "one approach is to find a way to make it exact" for the neuro-symbolic guarantee
  problem) rather than a specific, concrete technique with its own specific limitation.
- In Task C, writing a generic "my topic is related to open problems in general" connection
  instead of a specific scope assessment — the exercise requires explicitly judging *too large*,
  *too small*, or *appropriately scoped*, with a stated reason, not just acknowledging a vague
  relationship exists.

**Debugging tip (for writing, not code):** if Task A's restatement and the lecture content's
original wording would be hard for a third party to tell apart, that is a sign to revise rather
than submit.

**Instructor tip:** this lab pairs well with a short peer-exchange step (not formally required by
the rubric) where students read a partner's Task C scope assessment and independently guess
whether they would judge the same problem statement "too large," "too small," or "appropriately
scoped" — a mismatch is a useful discussion prompt heading into Week 15's workshop.
