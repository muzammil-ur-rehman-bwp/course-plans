# Week 16 — Lecture Content: Capstone Presentations; Course Review

## 1. The Defense Format
Each student presents and defends their research proposal in a qualifying-exam/thesis-proposal-
defense format (12–15 minutes + extended committee-style Q&A — see
`presentations/capstone-presentation-template.md`): title and pillar, problem statement, related-
work survey, proposed novel approach, feasibility argument or preliminary results, anticipated
risks, then committee questioning from peers and the instructor. This is evaluated as a thesis
committee would evaluate a genuine proposal — the object being judged is the soundness of a
**plan** for research not yet completed, not a finished, polished piece of work (see
`assignments/capstone-rubric.md`).

## 2. Course Map Recap
The semester's progression, as a single connected argument rather than sixteen separate topics:
**expressive logics** (higher-order logic/type theory responding to FOL's inability to quantify
over predicates; many-valued and paraconsistent logics responding to classical logic's inability
to handle genuine incompleteness/inconsistency; SROIQ responding to ALC's insufficient
expressiveness for real OWL 2 DL ontologies) → **structured and quantitative non-classical
reasoning** (ASPIC+ giving Dung's abstract arguments internal structure; the distribution
semantics giving probabilistic reasoning an independent-fact, program-oriented alternative to
MLNs' weighted-formula view) → **neuro-symbolic integration and formal verification** (
differentiable/fuzzy logic and neural theorem proving as a still-unsettled attempt to make
symbolic reasoning learnable; model checking as a rigorous decision procedure built on the
graduate course's LTL) → **multi-agent, explanatory, and methodological topics** (belief merging
extending single-agent AGM to peer agents; justification research treating "why does this
follow?" itself as an open problem; ontology evolution as a grounded engineering challenge;
research methods and the open-problems survey, closing on the honest acknowledgment that this
landscape remains unfinished).

## 3. Connecting Back to the Graduate Foundations
Every pillar in this course is a direct extension of something the graduate course established
as settled: SROIQ from ALC, ASPIC+ from Dung's AF, the distribution semantics from MLNs' broader
statistical-relational project, model checking from LTL, belief merging from AGM, and
justification research from proof trees. None of this course's material stands alone — each week
is legible only against the graduate-course foundation it builds past, which is exactly why that
foundation was never re-taught here: it was already load-bearing from Week 1 onward.

## 4. Connecting Forward to Where KR Research Is Headed
The Week 14 open-problems survey is this course's deliberately unresolved closing word: scaling
expressive reasoning and justification-finding without losing explainability; integrating
probabilistic and paraconsistent semantics into one coherent account of uncertain, inconsistent
information; and closing the gap between neuro-symbolic systems' empirical success and any
rigorous guarantee they respect the symbolic constraints they were trained toward. None of these
are solved by this course, and none are presented as solved — a postgraduate KR course's honest
final word is that the field's frontier keeps moving, and the proposal each student just defended
is one small, well-scoped attempt to move it a little further.

## 5. Closing Discussion
In the final session, discuss as a class: which of the three Week 14 open problems did the most
capstone proposals this semester actually engage with, and what does that pattern suggest about
where this specific cohort's research interests and this field's current center of gravity
overlap? This is a discussion prompt, not a graded exercise — its purpose is to send students into
further KR research (a thesis, a PhD, or further coursework) with a concrete sense of the
field's actual current shape, not just this course's syllabus.
