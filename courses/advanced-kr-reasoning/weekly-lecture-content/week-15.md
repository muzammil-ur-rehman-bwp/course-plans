# Week 15 — Lecture Content: Research Proposal Work Session

## 1. Purpose of This Session
This week has no new technical content — it is structured, instructor-guided time to complete and
pressure-test all four required components of the capstone research proposal before the Week 16
defense: the problem statement, the related-work survey, the proposed novel approach, and the
feasibility argument or preliminary results. The content below is guidance for how to use the
session well, not new material to learn.

## 2. Re-Applying the Precision Test, One More Time
Before the workshop, re-run the Week 13 precision test on your current problem statement: could a
peer who has not seen your proposal state, from the statement alone, what evidence would resolve
it? If the statement has drifted back toward naming a topic rather than a gap during drafting
(a common failure mode under deadline pressure — vague language creeps back in because it is
easier to write), fix this *before* the workshop, since peer reviewers will spend their limited
time on deeper issues if the statement itself is already precise.

## 3. Synthesizing the Survey, Not Just Listing It
Re-check the related-work survey against the Week 13 standard: does it *relate* the 5+ papers to
each other (who builds on whom, where they agree or conflict), and does it end by using that
synthesis to motivate the stated gap? A common failure at this stage is a survey that reads as
five accurate, well-written, but entirely independent paragraphs — each paper correctly
summarized, with no sentence anywhere connecting them. This is the single most common reason a
draft proposal under-delivers relative to the quality of research behind it.

## 4. The Feasibility Argument's Non-Negotiable Structure
Whether or not a pilot was run, the feasibility argument must explicitly state: (1) what must be
true for the proposed approach to work; (2) the **single most likely way it could fail** — named
honestly, specifically, and without minimizing it; (3) a reasoned case for why that named risk
does not make the approach obviously doomed. An argument that only lists reasons the approach will
work, with no named risk, has not met this standard — reviewers (here, peers in the workshop; in
Week 16, the full committee) will ask for the risk directly if it is missing, so naming it
proactively is strictly better than being asked.

## 5. The Peer-Review Workshop Format
Each draft proposal receives direct, specific critique from peers role-playing a thesis-proposal
committee, on all four components, in two rotations with different reviewers (so a draft gets two
independent sets of committee-style questions, not one). The goal is not polish or encouragement
— it is to surface, now, the same kind of pointed question the real Week 16 committee will ask,
while there is still time to respond. "The survey doesn't actually show these two papers
conflict, even though you say they do" is exactly the kind of workshop feedback that is far more
valuable than "looks good."

## 6. Producing a Revision Plan
After both workshop rotations, write a short, concrete revision plan: for each piece of specific
feedback received, state exactly what will change in the proposal before Week 16, or — if the
feedback will not be acted on — a specific, defensible reason why not (e.g., "reviewer suggested
narrowing the survey to only the three most recent papers; declining, because the two older
papers are needed to show the historical disagreement the gap depends on"). A revision plan that
only restates the feedback without committing to a specific response is not sufficient.

## 7. In-Class/Lab Exercise
Complete both workshop rotations as reviewer and as presenter. As a reviewer, ask at least one
question targeting the problem statement's precision and at least one targeting the survey's
synthesis (not just its accuracy) for each proposal reviewed. As a presenter, write your revision
plan immediately after your second rotation, while the specific feedback is still fresh, rather
than from memory later.
