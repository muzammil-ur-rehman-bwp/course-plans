# Research Capstone — Grading Rubric

**Due:** Draft proposal Week 15; final proposal + oral defense Week 16 | **Weight:** 40% of
course grade total

| Component | Weight (of 40%) | Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Problem Statement | 5% | Precise and falsifiable (passes the Week 13 precision test); genuinely open, not already settled by material covered in this course or in the student's own survey |
| Related-Work Survey (5+ papers) | 8% | Each paper accurately represented (claim and finding, not title/abstract paraphrase); papers related to each other, not summarized in isolation; survey ends by motivating the specific stated gap |
| Proposed Novel Approach | 8% | The student's own formulation; clearly distinguishable from any single surveyed paper's contribution; a defensible (even if ambitious) extension or combination of existing ideas |
| Feasibility Argument / Preliminary Results | 10% | States explicitly what must be true for the approach to work, names the main technical risk honestly, and gives a reasoned case for why the risk is manageable — or, where a pilot was run, reports results honestly (including negative/partial ones) with multiple-seed spread for any stochastic method |
| Written Proposal Quality | 4% | Clear structure (problem statement, related work, approach, feasibility/results); properly cited sources; writing precise enough to be attacked technically |
| Oral Defense | 5% | Clear qualifying-exam-style talk within time; accurate, honest responses under committee-style questioning, including appropriately saying "the survey doesn't settle that" where true |
| **Total** | **40%** | |

## Grading Notes
- This is a **proposal-style** rubric, not a completed-project rubric: a proposal with no
  experimental results at all, but a precise problem statement, an accurately synthesized survey,
  a genuinely novel approach, and an honest feasibility argument, can score as well as — or better
  than — a proposal that includes a small pilot but a weak survey or an unfalsifiable problem
  statement. The soundness of the research plan matters more than whether anything was run.
- A feasibility argument that only lists reasons the approach will work, with no named risk, has
  not met the Week 13 standard and cannot receive full credit on that component regardless of how
  well-written the rest of the proposal is.
- Reproducing or extending a published idea (even approximately, at smaller scale) as part of the
  proposed approach is explicitly encouraged and does not count against originality; presenting a
  cited paper's reported results as the student's own preliminary findings, without having run
  the experiment, is an academic-integrity violation (see `course-plan.md` §10), not merely a
  rubric deduction.
- Any disclosed collaboration (e.g., peer feedback received during the Week 13/15 workshops) is
  expected and does not count against the individual-work requirement; undisclosed collaboration
  on the problem statement, survey, approach, or feasibility reasoning does.
- A proposal whose claimed novelty rests on a misunderstanding of a well-established result this
  course covers (for example, claiming to be first to observe that NTK training involves no
  feature learning, which Week 2 already establishes) is marked down under Related-Work Survey
  and Proposed Novel Approach alike — the survey should have caught it, and the approach is not
  actually novel relative to this course's own assumed background.
