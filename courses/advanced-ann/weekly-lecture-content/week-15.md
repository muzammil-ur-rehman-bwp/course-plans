# Week 15 — Lecture Content: Research Proposal Work Session

## 1. Purpose of This Week
By Week 15, every student has a complete first draft of all four required proposal components
(problem statement, 5+ paper related-work survey, proposed novel approach, feasibility argument or
preliminary results — see `assignments/capstone-proposal-guidelines.md`). This week has no new
theoretical content; it is structured time to subject that draft to the same rigor this course has
applied to every published result it has covered, applied now to the student's own work, via a
thesis-committee-style peer-review workshop.

## 2. What a Thesis-Committee-Style Review Looks For
A reviewer (peer or instructor) applies, to someone else's draft, exactly the standards Week 13
introduced for reading others' papers and scoping one's own proposal:
- **Problem statement:** apply the precision test directly — read only the problem statement, and
  try to state what evidence would resolve it. If this cannot be done, the statement needs
  revision, regardless of how interesting the surrounding topic is.
- **Related-work survey:** check specifically for synthesis, not just accuracy — do the five-plus
  summaries relate to each other (who builds on whom, where they agree/conflict), and does the
  survey end by stating a specific gap the problem statement targets?
- **Proposed approach:** check that it is distinguishable from simply restating one surveyed
  paper's contribution — a reviewer should be able to point to what, specifically, is the
  student's own formulation versus what is directly inherited from a cited source.
- **Feasibility argument:** check specifically that a real risk is named, not just reasons for
  optimism — a feasibility section that reads entirely as "this should work because..." without a
  stated risk has not met the Week 13 standard.

## 3. Giving Useful Feedback
Useful peer feedback is **specific and actionable**, not generic praise or generic criticism:
"your survey doesn't relate the papers to each other — specifically, you summarize [paper 2] and
[paper 4] separately but they report conflicting findings on [X], and your draft doesn't say so or
explain why" is useful; "make the survey better" is not. Reviewers write feedback keyed to each of
the four components separately, using the peer-review structured feedback form, which mirrors
`assignments/capstone-rubric.md`'s weighting so feedback effort is allocated the way the final
grade will be.

## 4. Receiving Feedback and Writing a Revision Plan
Receiving specific, critical feedback on one's own proposed research question is a core
postgraduate skill in its own right — the correct response is neither to dismiss it nor to accept
every suggestion uncritically, but to evaluate each piece of feedback on its merits and produce a
short written revision plan: for each piece of feedback received, state whether and how you will
address it (or, if you disagree, state why, specifically). This revision plan is not separately
graded but is expected to visibly inform the Week 16 final submission; a final proposal that does
not address substantive, specific Week 15 feedback at all is a weaker submission under
`assignments/capstone-rubric.md`'s written-proposal-quality criterion.

## 5. Any Disclosed Collaboration
Per `course-plan.md` §10, this peer-review collaboration is expected and must be disclosed in the
final proposal's acknowledgments — this is not an academic-integrity concern, provided the
disclosure is made; only *undisclosed* collaboration on the problem statement, survey, approach, or
feasibility reasoning is a concern.

## 6. Work-Session Exercise
See `lab-manuals/lab-15.md`: complete a full round of structured peer review (give and receive),
and produce a written revision plan addressing every piece of specific feedback received before
the end of the session.
