# Lab Notes 13 — Problem Statement Drafting & Related-Work Annotation

**Concept recap:** a problem statement is a question, not a topic — the precision test asks
whether a knowledgeable reader could state, from the statement alone, what evidence would
resolve it; a postgraduate-level survey must represent each paper accurately, relate papers to
each other, and use the synthesis to motivate a specific, stated gap (Week 13, §§1–2).

**Common pitfalls:**
- Writing a problem statement that is really still a topic dressed up as a question — e.g., "How
  can we improve bandit algorithms?" is not meaningfully more falsifiable than "I want to study
  bandit algorithms"; the Week 13 example rewrite (noise-robust UCB confidence bounds) shows the
  level of specificity actually required.
- Annotating a paper from its title or abstract alone rather than having actually read enough of
  it to state its specific claim and finding accurately — a postgraduate survey standard
  explicitly requires this (Week 13, §2.1), and Task D annotations that are inaccurate paraphrases
  cannot receive credit regardless of how polished the writing is.
- In Task B, giving critique that is encouraging but not actionable ("sounds interesting!")
  rather than applying the precision test's specific question — this is the same "weak feedback"
  failure mode Week 15's peer-review protocol will name explicitly; practice specificity now,
  since it is directly graded here too.
- Treating Task C's revision as cosmetic wording changes rather than an actual substantive
  response to the specific critique received — the revision must trace back to the critique, not
  simply restate the original statement in different words.

**Debugging tip:** apply the precision test to your own draft yourself, before trading with a
peer — if you cannot state what evidence would resolve your own question, no peer critique is
needed to already know it is not yet precise enough.

**Instructor tip:** require candidate papers (title/venue/year) to be submitted before the lab
session begins, so that no student can stall Task D by not yet having chosen any — the annotation
exercise is most valuable when it forces engagement with papers the student has already
committed to, not a last-minute search during the lab itself.
