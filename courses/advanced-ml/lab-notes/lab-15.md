# Lab Notes 15 — Capstone Peer-Review Workshop

**Concept recap:** the thesis-committee-style peer-feedback protocol asks four specific
questions about a full draft proposal (problem-statement precision/openness; survey accuracy and
synthesis; genuine novelty of the approach; honesty of the feasibility argument's named risk),
each answered with a justification, not a rating.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Giving feedback as an overall impression ("looks good," "needs work") instead of answering each
  of the four questions separately with a specific justification — the protocol exists precisely
  to force specificity on exactly the dimensions that matter for the capstone rubric.
- Skipping the spot-check in Question 2 (verifying at least one cited paper's summary against the
  paper itself) — this is the single most valuable, and most often skipped, part of the review;
  do not substitute "the survey reads well" for actually checking one citation.
- In Task C, treating all received feedback as automatically correct — a reviewer can
  misunderstand a technical point; disagreement is legitimate when argued, and simply accepting
  every critique without evaluation is not genuine triage.
- Writing a Task D revision plan in vague terms ("improve the survey") rather than a specific,
  checkable action — see Week 15 §4's worked example of the level of specificity expected.

**Debugging tip:** before giving feedback on Question 4 (feasibility argument), check specifically
whether a named risk is present at all — a draft that only lists reasons the approach will work,
with no risk named, has already failed this question regardless of anything else in the review.

**Instructor tip:** circulate anonymized examples of strong and weak Task D revision plans from a
prior offering (or construct illustrative ones) before students write their own — concreteness is
easier to recognize in an example than to produce cold.
