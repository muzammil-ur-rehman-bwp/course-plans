# Lab Notes 14 — Frontier Paper Mini-Investigation

**Concept recap:** extracting a paper's actual problem statement (not its title or abstract) and
classifying its claims by evidentiary support are direct rehearsals of the skills a capstone
proposal's related-work survey requires.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Restating the paper's abstract as the "problem statement" in Task B — the abstract often states
  the paper's *contribution*, not the precise question it set out to answer; these can differ,
  and the distinction matters for the precision test.
- In Task C, forcing the paper into one of the three Week 14 open-question shapes when it
  genuinely does not fit — explicitly stating "this fits none of the three, because..." is a
  valid and expected answer for some papers, not a sign of an incomplete analysis.
- In Task D, classifying a claim as "evidence-supported" when the paper's own experiment uses a
  different setting, scale, or assumption than the claim's stated generality — this is exactly
  the "assumptions vs. claimed generality" mismatch the Week 13 reading framework warns against,
  now applied to someone else's paper rather than the student's own problem statement.

**Debugging tip:** before finalizing Task D, re-read the paper's own limitations or discussion
section (if present) — authors often flag exactly which of their claims are more speculative
themselves, which is useful (though not sufficient on its own) corroboration for your
classification.

**Instructor tip:** assign papers from genuinely different pillars across the class and have a
few students present their Task C mapping to the full group — comparing how differently the same
open-question-shape framework applies across pillars reinforces that the framework is general,
not specific to one topic.
