# Lab Notes 13 — Problem-Statement Drafting & Related-Work Annotation

**Concept recap:** a problem statement must pass the precision test (a knowledgeable reader can
state what evidence would resolve it, from the statement alone); a related-work annotation must
record what a paper actually claims and found, not a title/abstract paraphrase.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Drafting a problem statement that is really a topic with a question mark appended (e.g., "Can
  we improve robust estimation?") — this fails the precision test just as surely as a bare topic
  statement; a precise version names the specific setting, the specific prior result being
  challenged or extended, and what evidence (a rate, a counterexample, an experiment outcome)
  would resolve it.
- In Task B, giving feedback that only says "this is good" or "this is vague" without applying
  the precision test explicitly — record the critique as an answer to the test's actual question
  ("could I state what evidence would resolve this, from the statement alone? here is what I
  could or couldn't state"), not a general impression.
- In Task D, annotating a paper from its abstract alone without reading enough of the paper to
  state its actual finding accurately — this is exactly the failure mode the three-part survey
  standard (Week 13, §3) is designed to catch, and annotations done this way will need to be
  redone before the Week 15 draft.

**Debugging tip:** for your own Task A draft, try stating out loud what evidence would resolve
your own problem statement, before showing it to a peer — if you cannot do this for your own
statement, it is not yet ready for peer critique.

**Instructor tip:** collect Task A drafts before Task B's exchange and spot-check a few against
the precision test yourself — mismatches between instructor and peer assessments of the same
draft are a productive discussion point about how precisely the test should be applied.
