# Research Capstone — Grading Rubric

**Due:** Draft paper Week 15; final paper + presentation Week 16 | **Weight:** 25% of course
grade total (includes proposal, literature review, experiment, paper, and presentation)

| Component | Weight (of 25%) | Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Proposal | 2% | Specific motivating question, genuine candidate literature, feasible and concretely scoped experiment/derivation plan |
| Literature Review | 5% | 3–5 relevant papers accurately summarized (problem, method, result) using the Week 15 critique checklist; ends with an explicit stated gap/question the experiment addresses, not just a list of summaries |
| Experiment/Derivation Design & Execution | 6% | Appropriate benchmark/task or derivation target; fair, clearly stated baseline; multiple random seeds reported with spread for any stochastic method (per Week 15) |
| Results & Analysis | 5% | Results reported honestly, including negative/partial results; analysis correctly interprets what the results do and do not show, and does not overclaim beyond the evidence |
| Written Paper | 4% | Clear conference-short-paper structure (introduction, related work, method, results, limitations); properly cited sources, correctly attributing any named theoretical result discussed |
| Presentation | 3% | Clear conference-style talk within time; results shown where applicable; accurate, honest Q&A responses |

## Grading Notes
- This is a **research-style** rubric: a project that honestly reports a negative or partial
  result, correctly analyzed against a sound experiment design, can score as well as — or better
  than — a project with impressive-looking results but a weak literature review, no baseline, or
  single-run "evidence." The soundness of the literature review and experimental design matters
  as much as the raw outcome.
- Reproducing a published result or derivation (even approximately, at smaller scale) is
  explicitly encouraged and does not count against originality; presenting someone else's
  reported results as your own experimental findings, without actually running the experiment, is
  an academic integrity violation (see `course-plan.md` §10).
- Any named theoretical result discussed (e.g., the Neural Tangent Kernel, the Lottery Ticket
  Hypothesis) must be correctly attributed to its actual authors; the written paper is graded down
  for misattribution exactly as it would be for a factual error.
- Pairs must clearly attribute each member's contribution in the written paper; grading can differ
  between partners if contributions were significantly uneven.
