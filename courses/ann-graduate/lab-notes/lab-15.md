# Lab Notes 15 — Paper Critique and Capstone Reproducibility Assessment

**Concept recap:** this lab applies Week 15's critique checklist and reproducibility framework to
the student's *own* capstone material — it is a direct, graded dry run of the literature-review
and experiment-design rigor the final capstone paper and presentation will be judged on.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Writing a Task A critique that only *summarizes* a paper (what it did) without stating the
  four required elements (claim, assumptions, evidence strength, limitations) — a summary is not
  a critique; the limitations element in particular is frequently skipped because it requires
  more independent judgment than the others.
- Writing a Task B gap statement that restates "I want to study X" rather than identifying a
  specific question the candidate papers leave open — this is the single most common weakness
  instructors flag at the capstone-proposal stage, and this lab exists to catch it earlier.
- In Task C, naming a reproducibility plan that reports only a single run's number with no
  seed/spread plan at all, especially for anything involving random initialization or stochastic
  optimization — by Week 15, "report multiple seeds with spread" should be an automatic habit,
  not an afterthought.
- In Task D, naming a risk that is purely about *time management* (e.g., "I might not finish")
  rather than about the *experiment's scientific validity* (e.g., "if both conditions perform
  identically, I won't be able to distinguish X from Y without a larger dataset") — the latter is
  what this task is asking for.

**Debugging tip:** if a student's Task B gap statement still looks like a restated topic rather
than a specific question, ask them directly: "what number or outcome, if you saw it, would tell
you the answer?" — if they cannot answer, the gap statement is not yet specific enough.

**Instructor tip:** treat this lab's output as an early-warning check on every capstone's
viability — a weak Task B or C here reliably predicts a weak capstone literature review or
experiment design later, and is far cheaper to fix now than after the Week 16 presentation.
