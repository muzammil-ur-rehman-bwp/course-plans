# Capstone Presentation Template (Student-Facing)

**Target length:** 7–10 minutes + Q&A (conference-talk format) | **Given to students in Week 7–8
alongside the proposal guidelines, used for the Week 15 practice talk and the Week 16
presentation.**

## Required Slides
1. **Title** — project title, your name(s), one-line subtopic (e.g., "Advanced Search").
2. **Problem statement** — what question is this project investigating, and why does it matter?
3. **Related work** — your 3–5-paper literature review, compressed to the key prior results and
   the gap your project addresses.
4. **Method/Experiment** — what you reproduced or extended, and exactly how (benchmark/task,
   baseline, ablation if applicable).
5. **Results** — your findings, with at least one chart/table; report multiple-seed spread for
   any stochastic method, not a single run's number.
6. **Analysis** — what the results actually show (and do not show) — honest interpretation, not
   just a restatement of the numbers.
7. **Limitations & next steps** — at least one genuine limitation and what you would try with
   more time/compute.
8. **Questions** — invite audience questions.

## Presentation Tips
- This is a **conference-talk format**, not a classroom book report — lead with the question
  your project investigates and why it matters to someone in the field, not with "today I will
  talk about..."
- Practice the timing — 7–10 minutes goes faster than it sounds; cut detail, not clarity.
- Show your literature review's stated gap explicitly — the audience should understand, within
  the first 2–3 minutes, not just what you did but why it was worth doing given what was already
  known.
- Report results with their spread (multiple seeds), not a single cherry-pickable number — this
  directly reflects the Week 13 statistical-significance guidance and is part of the grading
  rubric (`assignments/capstone-rubric.md`).
- Be honest about limitations — the rubric explicitly rewards sound analysis of what did not
  work, or what remains uncertain, over simply claiming success.
- In Q&A, "I don't know, the data doesn't show that" is a better answer than an overstated guess.
